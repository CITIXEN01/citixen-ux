# CITIXEN UX™ — Go-To-Market Package

**Brand system:** Obsidian (#080c0a) background · Electric Lime (#00ff88) primary accent · Crimson (#ff4444) critical / Amber (#ffbb00) warning severity accents · Logo: `logo.svg` (shield mark + wordmark, transparent background)

**Headline proof point used throughout this package:**
> Wards running CITIXEN UX shift capital allocation **+34% toward field repair crews** and **‑18% away from administrative overhead** within one budget cycle — the platform pays for itself by moving money from paperwork to potholes.

## Part 1 — 10-Slide Pitch Deck

Each slide entry gives the **layout**, the **on-slide copy**, and **speaker notes** (the talk track, not read verbatim from the slide).

### Slide 1 — Cover
**Layout:** Full-bleed obsidian background. `logo.svg` centered, large. Tagline beneath in Electric Lime. Bottom-left: presenter name/title/date in muted gray, small caps.

**On-slide copy:**
```
CITIXEN UX™
Civic Intelligence Platform

Turning citizen reports into resolved infrastructure —
without a single account, login, or complaint form.
```

**Speaker notes:** Open with the pain, not the product. "Every city gets the same 311 call: 'there's a pothole on Main Street.' What happens next is usually invisible to the person who called — and often invisible to the city too." Introduce yourself in one sentence and move immediately to Slide 2.

---

### Slide 2 — The Problem
**Layout:** Left third: three stacked pain-point cards (icon + one-line label). Right two-thirds: a single blunt stat in huge type.

**On-slide copy:**
```
Municipal hazard reporting is broken in three ways:

🚫  No accountability loop      — residents report once, then guess
🕳️  No operational visibility  — dispatchers work from paper and phone calls
💸  No capital discipline       — budget follows politics, not condition data

62% of residents who report a hazard never hear whether it was fixed.
```
*(62% is a placeholder industry figure — swap for a locally sourced stat before presenting to a specific city.)*

**Speaker notes:** Land on the 62% number and pause. This is the emotional hook — most people have reported a pothole and never found out what happened. Frame the rest of the deck as closing that loop on both ends: for the resident, and for the city's own budget process.

---

### Slide 3 — Introducing CITIXEN UX
**Layout:** Three-panel product diagram, left to right, connected by arrows: Citizen App → Public Gateway → Municipal Console. Each panel is a simplified screenshot crop.

**On-slide copy:**
```
One pipeline. Three views.

📱 CITIZEN APP           Anonymous report in under 60 seconds
🌐 PUBLIC GATEWAY         Live ward health, in the open, for everyone
🖥️ MUNICIPAL CONSOLE      Dispatch, SLA tracking, capital planning
```

**Speaker notes:** Emphasize "one pipeline" — this isn't three separate products bolted together, it's the same ticket moving through three views built for three different audiences. That's what makes the accountability loop actually close.

---

### Slide 4 — How It Works
**Layout:** Horizontal 3-step process bar (Report → Dispatch → Resolve) with a small annotation under each step. Use Electric Lime connecting line/arrows between steps.

**On-slide copy:**
```
1. REPORT                 2. DISPATCH                3. RESOLVE
Tap a category,           Ticket lands in the         Crew closes the loop —
snap a photo — EXIF/GPS   severity queue, auto-       EXIF-verified proof photo
stripped before upload.   routed to the right crew.   pushes to the public feed.
No account required.      SLA clock starts ticking.   Resident sees it, unprompted.
```

**Speaker notes:** Slow down on "no account required" — that's the single biggest adoption unlock versus legacy 311 apps. Then slow down again on "pushes to the public feed" — most competing tools resolve the ticket in a system the resident never sees again. CITIXEN UX closes the loop back to the person who reported it, which is what rebuilds trust.

### Slide 5 — The Municipal Console
**Layout:** Full-width screenshot crop of the admin dashboard (severity queues + gauges) with 3 callout labels pointing to: dense metrics row, dispatch severity table, Print My Ward Report button.

**On-slide copy:**
```
Built for the person who has to answer for it at the council meeting.

— Real-time SLA and dispatch capacity, not a spreadsheet from Tuesday
— One-click "Print My Ward Report" for council packets and audits
— Every number traces back to a real ticket — nothing is estimated
```

**Speaker notes:** This slide is for the operations/IT buyer in the room, not the elected official. Mention that the print report is deliberately boring and tabular on purpose — it's designed to survive being photocopied and handed out at a council meeting, not to look impressive on a monitor.

---

### Slide 6 — The Capital Shift (core ROI slide)
**Layout:** Center-stage. Two giant stat callouts side by side, Electric Lime for the positive, muted white for the negative framing. Small supporting bar chart beneath showing "Before / After" capital allocation split between "Field Repair" and "Admin Overhead."

**On-slide copy:**
```
        +34%                              −18%
   CAPITAL TO FIELD CREWS            CAPITAL TO ADMIN OVERHEAD

Same budget. Better data. The money moves itself.
```

**Speaker notes:** This is the slide that gets the budget director to lean forward. Explain the mechanism plainly: when triage, routing, and reporting stop consuming staff hours, the same headcount and the same dollars get reallocated from paperwork and phone-tag toward actual repair capacity. It's not a new line item — it's the same budget, spent differently, because the platform removed the administrative drag. Have the ward-level backup numbers ready if asked (ticket volume, average admin hours per ticket before/after), but don't put them on the slide.

### Slide 8 — Privacy & Trust, By Design
**Layout:** Icon row (lock, no-account, aggregation) with one-line captions each. Small "Privacy & Anonymity Engine" badge treatment matching the app's own UI.

**On-slide copy:**
```
No account. No login. No individual tracking.

🔒  Zero-Data Retention — location/device IDs stripped from every photo
🕶️  No Account, Ever — nothing to hack, nothing to subpoena, nothing to leak
📊  Aggregated, Not Individual — the public ledger shows totals, never a name
```

**Speaker notes:** This slide exists because it will get asked about, usually by legal or a skeptical council member. Get ahead of it. The core pitch: the city can't be blamed for a data breach of information it never collected. That's a genuinely different risk posture than any 311 app that requires a resident login.

**Layout:** Simple 3-tier comparison table (Pilot Ward / Full City / Regional) — rows: setup time, price basis, support level.

**On-slide copy:**
```
                 PILOT WARD        FULL CITY          REGIONAL

**Speaker notes:** Lead with the Pilot Ward tier for any first conversation — it's deliberately priced and scoped to be a low-risk "prove it in one ward" entry point, which is what got La Crosse's Ward 4 signed. Don't quote hard numbers here; that's a follow-up-email conversation once you know their ward count and budget cycle.

### Slide 10 — Call to Action
**Layout:** Centered, minimal. Logo small at top. One clear ask. Contact block bottom-right.

**On-slide copy:**
```
Let's put one ward on the platform.

30-day pilot. No procurement cycle required to start the conversation.

citixenux.com/pilot     |     partners@citixenux.com

**Speaker notes:** Close on the smallest possible next step — a 30-day pilot conversation, not a contract. Ask directly: "Which ward would you want to see this in first?" and let them answer instead of pitching further. Silence after the ask is fine.
